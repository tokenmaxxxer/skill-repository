# Subtraction: what to cut from a campaign

Research trail: Adams, Converse, Hales & Klotz, "People systematically
overlook subtractive changes" (Nature 594, 2021) on subtraction neglect,
plus Medium's minimalist-marketing surveys (Hritik Chhangani, Presslink
Media) on claim/CTA reduction, fetched this session — the subtraction-
neglect finding is general-cognition research applied here to campaign
review practice, flagged as such rather than presented as marketing-
native research.

## Rules

1. When drafting a campaign brief, cap the message to one core benefit
   and one CTA rather than including every true claim you could make,
   since minimalist-messaging practice finds each additional competing
   claim directly increases decision paralysis and dilutes the one idea
   meant to land. source: https://medium.com/@hritik_chhangani/the-art-of-minimalism-how-less-is-more-revolutionizes-marketing-2ac672b54a4d

2. When reviewing a draft asset (page, email, ad) before launch, add an
   explicit "what can we remove" pass separate from the "what's
   missing" pass, since people systematically default to additive
   changes and overlook subtractive ones even when removal would
   improve the object — a review that only asks what's missing will
   never surface a cut. source:
   https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y

3. When a channel, segment, or claim has run a full review cycle with no
   measurable contribution and no identified upstream effect, cut it
   from the plan rather than keeping it on the assumption that removing
   it is riskier than keeping it — subtraction-neglect research shows
   this asymmetry (overweighting removal risk vs. the cost of keeping)
   is a bias to correct for, not a safe default. source:
   https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y

4. When two campaign assets serve overlapping segments with overlapping
   messages, merge them into one asset rather than running both, since
   redundant simultaneous claims compete for the same attention and
   neither benefits from the other's presence — the same "cap to one
   core benefit" logic in rule 1 applies across assets, not just within
   one. source: https://medium.com/@hritik_chhangani/the-art-of-minimalism-how-less-is-more-revolutionizes-marketing-2ac672b54a4d

5. **REMOVAL**: When an approved brand element (a tagline, a proof
   point, a channel) stops matching current positioning, remove it from
   active use immediately rather than leaving it live "just in case a
   future campaign needs it" — a kept-but-unused element still adds to
   a reader's cognitive load, and leaving it live is exactly the
   additive default the subtraction-neglect finding warns against.
   source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03380-y

6. When a message includes an emotional appeal that only weakly connects
   to the audience's stated values, drop it in favor of one stronger
   pathos element per [[message-persuasion]] rule 3, rather than
   stacking several weak emotional appeals — stacking does not
   substitute for choosing the right one, per pathos appealing
   specifically to the audience's own values. source:
   https://rdp.co.uk/being-persuasive-what-we-can-learn-from-aristotle-about-marketing/
